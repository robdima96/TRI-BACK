[16] Abstract
[17] Most Canadians experience low back pain (LBP) at some point in their life, but only some cases require emergency escalation. Constrained primary care access has led to more than 50,000 emergency department (ED) visits for LBP per year in British Columbia (BC), growing by about 5% each year. Artificial intelligence (AI) triage chatbots are already used by patients for health advice, but current tools perform poorly for musculoskeletal (MSK) conditions such as LBP – better tools are needed that are trustworthy enough to influence care-seeking behavior. Making triage reasoning visible may support trust in competence rather than in human-like presentation, but we don’t know what information patients want or need. This mixed-methods patient co-design study will randomize 108 adults who have sought care for acute LBP to one of three AI chatbot prototypes (n = 36 each). The prototypes share the same interface and curated clinical knowledge. Only transparency differs: (i) triage disposition only; (ii) a step-by-step chain-of-thought (CoT)/ReAct explanation of how the disposition was reached; or (iii) a visible traversal of the knowledge graph. After a baseline survey and an independent standardized LBP scenario, participants will rate cognitive trust and intention to follow the triage advice. Fifteen Phase 1 participants will be invited to Phase 2 think-aloud interviews and consensus methods to refine the platform until the mean System Usability Scale (SUS) score is at least 80.
[18] This research is designed and managed independently by the investigators. Financial support is provided by a publicly funded grant from the Government of Canada’s New Frontiers in Research Fund.
[19] 1. Background and Rationale
[20] Most Canadians will experience LBP over the life course; lifetime prevalence estimates are commonly cited as high as 80% (1). A substantial proportion of acute episodes are non-specific, meaning no obvious structural pathology adequately explains symptoms. Acute presentations can still be distressing and require triage to distinguish benign patterns from conditions needing urgent investigation (for example, malignancy, infection, fracture, or cauda equina syndrome). In Canada’s primary care access crisis, many people lack a regular provider to coordinate care, making walk-in settings such as the ED or urgent care the only point of contact. LBP is among the leading reasons for ED attendance, but when patients arrive, they encounter services that must prioritize higher-acuity presentations, and clinicians who have limited time, training, or expertise to deliver best-practice MSK care. Consequences can include unnecessary imaging, inappropriate opioid prescribing, fragmented follow-up, and dissatisfied patients.
[21] When timely in-person assessment is not available, people turn to AI chatbots for health advice (2). Tools designed for MSK triage remain limited and perform poorly, with sensitivity and specificity ranging from 39% to 91% and 23% to 80%, respectively (3). Better tools alone won’t be enough - advice is unlikely to change care-seeking behavior unless patients perceive the tool as trustworthy (4). This is especially relevant among people with MSK pain, who often have a history of symptoms being dismissed (5) – this makes trust a precondition for any virtual system intended to reduce avoidable ED visits. Significant effort has been undertaken in achieving a more human-like experience in artificial healthcare systems, under the hypothesis that this may increase patients affinity towards them, resulting in improved engagement/trust and therefore, health outcomes. Convincing human-likeness has been linked to behavioral trust in non-clinical settings (6), but it may manufacture trust that is not warranted by the tool's actual reliability, blur accountability, and is inherently deceptive. Instead of relying on affective cues such as empathy, comfort, and social presence to manufacture trust in medical AI, it may be more effective to support cognitive trust—confidence in the system’s reasoning and in one’s ability to evaluate it (7)—by making clinical reasoning visible. When users can visualize the reasoning behind a recommendation, they gain a sense of agency that transforms raw algorithmic data into meaningful, trustworthy insights (8,9). There is strong evidence that health technology designed without patient input underperforms on usability, trust, and uptake, and that co-design surfaces actionable requirements that surveys alone cannot (10). This project treats patients as active co-designers and as the authoritative source of evidence on whether and how triage AI should be built to seem human, and on how transparency shapes trust. This study uses a custom-built, virtual chatbot prototype (11) to test whether graded transparency in triage reasoning increases cognitive trust and intention to follow AI-generated LBP triage advice and then works with the same patients as co-designers to refine the platform until it is ready for testing in live health care settings.
[23] 2. Study Aim, Objectives, and Research Questions
[24] Objectives
[25] Objective 1: Measure the effects of clinical-reasoning transparency on cognitive trust and intention to follow AI-generated LBP triage advice.
[26] Objective 2: Iteratively evaluate the usability of the virtual triage platform with patients as co-designers, to measure and refine readiness for testing in live health care settings.
[27] Hypotheses
[28] We hypothesize that (i) cognitive trust ratings will increase across low, medium, and high transparency prototypes; and (ii) baseline characteristics, attitudes toward AI, and digital health literacy will moderate the transparency–trust relationship.
[29] Research Questions
[30] Among adults who have sought care for an episode of acute LBP in BC, does increasing clinical-reasoning transparency correlate with higher ratings of cognitive trust and intention to follow AI-generated LBP triage advice after one clinical role-play scenario?
[31] Among adults who have sought care for an episode of acute LBP in BC, do baseline characteristics, including digital health literacy and attitudes toward AI, moderate the transparency–trust relationship?
[32] What design features do adult patients with LBP prioritize for a usable, trustworthy MSK virtual triage platform, and can iterative co-design achieve a mean SUS of at least 80?
[33] 3. Study Design and Methods
[34] Design
[35] This is a two-phase mixed-methods patient co-design study. Phase 1 is a between-subjects experiment. We will randomize 108 adults in BC who sought care for acute LBP to one of three prototypes (n = 36 per group). All three share the same interface and a curated set of clinical knowledge, which the chatbot searches to generate advice (retrieval-augmented generation / GraphRAG). Across groups, only transparency differs: (i) triage disposition only; (ii) a step-by-step CoT/ReAct explanation of how the disposition was reached; (iii) a visible traversal of the knowledge graph. Participants are told they are interacting with an AI-operated chatbot. Phase 2 recruits 15 Phase 1 participants—purposively selected for equity—into think-aloud interviews and consensus methods to refine and prioritize design features alongside iterative platform feasibility testing.
[37] Setting and Participants
[38] All study activities are conducted online and hosted from the DigiMSK laboratory at The University of British Columbia City Centre 1 (Surrey) site. Consent and questionnaires are administered online via REDCap. After online consent and the baseline survey, participants receive an access code for an independent web-based chatbot session (~15 minutes), followed by the remaining surveys within the same session.  Phase 2 think-aloud interviews and consensus sessions use the UBC licensed Zoom platform.
[39] Inclusion Criteria
[40] Adults (19+) who have sought care for an acute episode of LBP in BC at least once in the previous 12 months
[41] Able to participate in English in online study activities
[42] Exclusion Criteria
[43] Under 19 years of age
[44] Unable to participate in English in online study activities
[45] Target sample size
[46] We will recruit 108 participants for Phase 1 (36 per transparency condition). This sample provides 80% power to detect a between-arm difference of approximately 0.67 SD at a two-sided alpha of 0.05; 95% confidence intervals will be reported. Fifteen Phase 1 participants will be invited to Phase 2, which fits current guidance on theoretical saturation for iterative co-design sessions (12).
[47] Recruitment
[48] Patient recruitment will use convenience sampling via established networks of the core team and collaborators. We will post information about the study to Reach BC, Pain BC, Chronic Pain Network, the Canadian Arthritis Patient Alliance, physiotherapy and primary-care clinic networks, community notice boards, and the research team’s social media accounts. We will purposively sample a representative cohort in terms of sex, gender, age, ethnicity, disability, and rurality. We will supplement recruiting with snowball sampling, as needed. Participants may share the Letter of Information (LOI) and Informed Consent (Appendix A) with others who may meet eligibility. Appendix A explains how interested individuals may contact the research team directly; researchers will not receive names, contact information, or other identifying details of potential participants without those individuals’ permission, in line with BREB Third Party Recruitment Guidelines. Only people who self-initiate contact will be screened for eligibility. Eligible participants will receive the full LOI and provide written informed e-consent via REDCap before any study activity. Recruitment materials will use plain language to describe voluntary participation, eligibility, Phase 1 honoraria ($40/hour), and privacy protections. Participants will be told that they will interact with AI-operated chatbot prototypes.
[50] Procedures
[51] Phase 1
[52] Eligible participants complete informed e-consent and a baseline survey in REDCap (Appendix B): sociodemographic items, digital health literacy (HLS19-DIGI) (13), and attitudes toward AI (Short General Attitudes towards Artificial Intelligence Scale; GAAIS-10) (14). Participants are then randomized 1:1:1 to a transparency condition using REDCap’s randomization module and receive a unique access code for the web application.
[53] For their assigned chatbot prototypes:
[54] The participant receives the standard low back pain clinical scenario (Appendix C)
[55] The participant independently works through the scenario with the chatbot until a recommended disposition is reached (less than 15 minutes per prototype).
[56] After receiving a disposition from their assigned prototype, the participant completes a questionnaire (Appendix D) on cognitive trust, and whether they would follow the triage advice given by the chatbot.
[57] The chatbot application was developed by the study researchers (11). Encoder models and regular expressions extract clinical entities from participant input; retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) over a knowledge base grounds application programming interface (API) calls to a commercial large language model (Google Gemini, Vertex AI) (15). Participants are instructed not to enter real names, contact details, personal health numbers, or details of their own medical history. All three prototypes share identical user interface and back-end; text-based output is styled via system prompting to restrict affective tone and social presence. The only experimental manipulation is transparency level:
[58] LOW — no triage disposition explanation beyond the recommendation
[59] MEDIUM — CoT / ReAct text-based clinical reasoning visible
[60] HIGH — knowledge-graph traversal visible.
[61] Phase 2
[62] Fifteen Phase 1 participants will be invited on the basis of equity and Phase 1 responses. Sessions are virtually hosted co-facilitated focus groups (n=3; five participants each): think-aloud interviews and consensus methods will be used to refine and prioritize design features, including data collection, tone and conversation flow, and transparency methods. Discussions will extract co-design priorities and identify barriers to adherence and trust.
[63] Usability outcomes are satisfaction, intent to continue use, intent to follow advice, and the SUS. The platform will be iterated over multiple sessions until the platform receives a mean participant rating on the SUS of at least 80 points. Sessions will be audio-recorded for transcription and analysis.
[67] Data Analysis
[68] Statistical analysis will be conducted using STATA IC v.16 and Python v.3.13.5. Descriptive statistics will be reported overall and by transparency group. For Objective 1, we will test a pre-specified linear trend in cognitive trust across ordinal transparency levels. We will fit linear and logistic regression models for cognitive trust and intention to follow advice, coding transparency as an ordered factor, and will report unadjusted estimates and estimates adjusted for baseline survey covariates. In both models (trust and intent to follow advice as dependent variables), exploratory moderation analysis will be conducted with interaction terms. All analyses will use a two-sided α = 0.05. Effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals will be reported. For Objective 2, workshop and think-aloud transcripts will be analyzed thematically (inductive–deductive coding of transcripts and facilitator field notes). Feasibility and usability outcomes, including SUS, will be summarized descriptively across iterative co-design rounds.
[69] Data Management
[70] Data collected throughout this study:
[71] Signed consent forms (Appendix A)
[72] Baseline survey: sociodemographics, HLS19-DIGI, and GAAIS-10 (Appendix B)
[73] Post-scenario ratings of cognitive trust and intention to follow advice (Appendix D)
[74] De-identified chatbot transcripts and automatically logged engagement metrics
[75] Audio recordings and transcripts (Phase 2)
[76] Facilitator field notes and co-design outputs (Phase 2)
[77] SUS, satisfaction, intent to continue use, intent to follow triage advice (Phase 2)
[78] Identifiable participant information will be separated from analytic datasets using an encrypted study key. Only the Principal Investigator will have access to the key, and all data analytics will use the generated study id. All electronic study data, including the encrypted study key, will be stored on UBC ARC Chinook server (https://arc.ubc.ca/compute-storage/ubc-arc-chinook). Phase 2 sessions will be conducted virtually using the secure, enterprise-licensed UBC Zoom video conferencing platform, which complies with BC FIPPA regulations. Sessions will be recorded and transcribed using Zoom’s integrated AI-transcription tool. Immediately following each session, the research team will download the audio and transcript files to the secure, password-protected UBC ARC Chinook server. Once the transfer is verified, all corresponding cloud recordings, transcripts, and temporary files will be permanently deleted from Zoom. Transcripts will then be manually reviewed for accuracy, and de-identified prior to analysis. Electronic consent forms (Appendix A) and participant questionnaires (Appendix B & D) will be stored on a secure REDCap cloud-hosted server managed by the UBC Faculty of Medicine. All data will be retained for at least 5 years; there are no plans to destroy the data. Data will be stored on the UBC ARC Chinook secured network server in encrypted and password-protected format. Dr. Clare Ardern, Dr. Robert Dima, and Alex Haagaard will have access to participant data. Dr. James Wrightson will access de-identified quantitative datasets. Dr. Karim Khan will advise in a supervisory capacity.
[79] Third-party language-model processing
[80] The chatbot will transmit non-identifying prompt and completion text to a commercial large language model API to generate conversational output. No personally identifying information and no participant-specific clinical information will be included in any API request. Interactions use standardized, fictional clinical vignettes and role-play content only. Technical safeguards include input filtering of likely identifiers where feasible, study-specific temporary credentials rather than personal accounts, and storage of transcripts under study IDs on UBC-controlled Canadian servers. The informed consent form discloses that anonymized vignette dialogue may be processed by a third-party model provider.
[82] 5. Expected Outcomes and Significance
[83] In Phase 1, this study will produce quantitative evidence on whether clinical-reasoning transparency increases cognitive trust and intention to follow AI-generated LBP triage advice, and which baseline characteristics moderate these relationships.
[84] In Phase 2, this study will yield a patient-endorsed virtual triage platform iterated to a mean SUS of at least 80, with co-designed requirements for data collection, transparency methods, and conversation flow.
[85] Significance
[86] Avoidable ED use for LBP is an access failure. Digital health platforms have transformative potential, but only if systems are trustworthy and acceptable to patients. This project addresses critical questions about trust and explainable AI, and aims to deliver a fully functional, patient-endorsed virtual triage platform, ready for testing in clinical health care environments.
