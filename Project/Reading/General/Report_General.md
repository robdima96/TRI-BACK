## 1. Validation and impact analysis of prognostic clinical prediction rules for LBP is needed: a systematic review

**First Author:** Robin Haskins

**Summary:**

The search retrieved over 10,000 records and yielded 35 studies describing 30 prognostic clinical prediction rules for nonsurgical LBP. Most rules were still early “development-only” tools; only three had validation evidence highlighted by the reviewers (including the Cassandra rule and Flynn manipulation rules). **No study showed that applying a rule in practice improved patient outcomes or saved resources**, so the review warns against treating most rules as ready for bedside use.

**Date:** 2015-07-01
**Publication:** Journal of Clinical Epidemiology
**Volume:** 68
**Issue:** 7
**Pages:** 821-832
**DOI:** 10.1016/j.jclinepi.2015.02.003 <http://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2015.02.003>

## 2. Can screening instruments accurately determine poor outcome risk in adults with recent onset LBP? A systematic review and meta-analysis

**First Author:** Emma L. Karran

**Summary:**

Pooling studies on adults with LBP under three months old, the meta-analysis **found screening tools more informative for predicting future disability than future pain**. The Örebro questionnaire appeared among the stronger performers for some outcomes. The authors caution that misclassifying patients’ risk remains possible, so tools should not be treated as perfect predictors.

**Date:** 2017-01-19

**Volume:** 15
**Issue:** 1
**Pages:** 13

## 4. Digital and online symptom checkers and health assessment/triage services for urgent health problems: systematic review

**First Author:** Duncan Chambers

**Summary:**

Systematic review of digital and online symptom checkers for people seeking help with urgent problems. Twenty-nine publications (27 studies) were included. **Evidence on patient safety was weak; diagnostic accuracy varied across systems but was generally low; algorithm-based triage tended to be more risk-averse than health professionals**. There was very limited evidence on compliance with online triage advice. Satisfaction was often high but largely in uncontrolled studies. **Younger and more educated people were more likely to use these services**. The authors conclude the digital service was introduced against substantial uncertainty about impact on important outcomes, with equity implications.

**Date:** 2019-08-01
**Publication:** BMJ Open
**Volume:** 9
**Issue:** 8
**Pages:** e027743
**DOI:** 10.1136/bmjopen-2018-027743 <http://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2018-027743>

## 5. The Effectiveness of Digital Health Interventions in the Management of Musculoskeletal Conditions: Systematic Literature Review

**First Author:** Stephanie Hewitt

**Summary:**

Nineteen trials met inclusion criteria for digital programs targeting musculoskeletal conditions. **Nine trials reported statistically significant pain reductions, and ten of sixteen trials that measured function showed significant functional gains**. Because study designs and outcomes differed so much, the authors could not combine results into one pooled effect.

## 8. The diagnostic and triage accuracy of digital and online symptom checker tools: a systematic review

**First Author:** William Wallace

**Summary:**

After 177 retrieved studies, 10 met inclusion (real or simulated patients; online/digital symptom checkers). Primary **diagnostic accuracy was low across studies (range about 19–37.9%) and differed between checkers despite consistent symptom input**. Triage accuracy was typically higher than diagnostic accuracy (range about 48.8–90.1%) but still variable. The authors conclude **diagnostic and triage performance are variable and often weak, and that relying on symptom checkers could pose patient-safety risks unless large real-world studies and clearer regulation and implementation standards follow**.

**Date:** 2022-08-17
**Publication:** npj Digital Medicine
**Volume:** 5
**Issue:** 1
**Pages:** 118
**DOI:** 10.1038/s41746-022-00667-w <http://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-022-00667-w>

## 9. A scoping review on the use and usefulness of online symptom checkers and triage systems: How to proceed?

**First Author:** Anthony Pairon

**Summary:**

After screening 10,250 records, 28 publications were included. Users skew younger, female, more educated, and more technologically literate, with implications for equity and the digital divide. **Triage algorithms remain risk-averse, which strains accuracy; newer algorithms show mixed success**. **Reported effects on demand, access, literacy, and surveillance vary widely**. Patients and clinicians are generally positive, but heterogeneity across studies and systems limits transferability. **The authors call for more comparable methods, setting-specific evaluations, regulation and standardization of assessments, and patient-centered design.**

**Date:** 2023-01-06
**Publication:** Frontiers in Medicine
**Volume:** 9
**DOI:** 10.3389/fmed.2022.1040926 <http://doi.org/10.3389/fmed.2022.1040926>

## 11. Prognostic Models for Chronic LBP Outcomes in Primary Care Are at High Risk of Bias and Lack Validation—High-Quality Studies Are Needed: A Systematic Review

**First Author:** Yanyan Fu

**Summary:**

From more than 5,500 screened records, only ten studies (34 prediction models) met the inclusion criteria. Reported model discrimination (AUC) ranged from 0.48 to 0.84, calibration was rarely reported, and risk-of-bias assessment raised serious concerns. ## 12. Cracking the Chronic Pain code: A scoping review of Artificial Intelligence in Chronic Pain research

**First Author:** Md Asif Khan

**Summary:**

60 studies met the reviewers’ criteria. The **included work most often reported AI success at diagnosing or classifying chronic pain, while relatively few studies focused on treatment, rehabilitation, or biological mechanisms of pain**. Across papers, support vector machines, logistic regression, and random forests were the most common algorithm families. **The take-home: AI publications cluster on diagnosis/classification, not on the kinds of mechanism-focused or treatment research the authors argue is needed next**.

## 13. Machine learning in the diagnosis, management, and care of patients with LBP: a scoping review of the literature and future directions

**First Author:** Andreas Seas

**Summary:**

The scoping review catalogued 223 machine-learning papers on LBP (1988–2023). More than half targeted detecting LBP from data; neural networks showed up in 106 articles. Common model inputs were history, demographics, and labs (about two-thirds of studies), and newer work often added imaging. Yet **only eight studies reported external validation, only nine shared code, and the reviewers conclude that most papers fall short of best-practice ML reporting**—making most of the literature hard to reproduce or trust for real-world use.

## 16. Evidence for Digital Health Tools Designed to Support the Triage of Musculoskeletal Conditions in Primary, Urgent, and Emergency Care Settings: Scoping Review

**First Author:** Linda K. Truong

**Summary:**

34 studies (covering 37,509 patients) made the final map of digital triage tools for muscle and joint problems. Only a minority of tools were MSK-specific; most were generic symptom checkers. Reported sensitivity and specificity varied widely (roughly 39–91% and 23–80% across tools), and reported accuracy ranged from 33% to 98%. The bottom line: **few tools target MSK well, and many perform poorly on MSK populations, so digital output should support—but not replace—clinical judgment**.

## Overall synthesis (from Summaries, emphasizing bolded conclusions)

### i) Main findings

Across systematic and scoping reviews, **prognostic rules and models for low back pain (LBP) are numerous but rarely shown to change care or outcomes when used in practice**: no included study demonstrated that applying a rule improved patient outcomes or saved resources, and recent reviews still **do not recommend routine adoption until higher-quality development and external validation** exist; discrimination is often modest and calibration under-reported. **Screening tools appear more informative for future disability than for future pain**, with instruments such as the Örebro questionnaire among stronger candidates, though misclassification remains a concern. **Evidence on digital symptom checkers and online triage is weak on patient safety**; **diagnostic accuracy is generally low and variable (roughly 19–38% for top diagnosis)**, **triage accuracy somewhat higher but still inconsistent (roughly 49–90%)**, **algorithm-based triage tends to be more risk-averse than clinicians**, and **younger, more educated users are overrepresented**, raising equity concerns. **Reported effects on demand, access, literacy, and surveillance vary widely**, and **heterogeneity across studies and systems limits transferability**. Despite that, **digital musculoskeletal interventions often report statistically significant pain and/or function improvements** in trials, though effects cannot be pooled simply. **Stepped and risk-stratified care programs often fail to integrate social context, multimorbidity, prior treatment, and links between clinic, self-management, and community**.

**Artificial intelligence work in chronic pain and LBP clusters on diagnosis, detection, and classification** rather than treatment, mechanisms, or rehabilitation; **machine learning studies rarely report external validation or share code**, limiting reproducibility and trust. **Large language models can show high diagnostic accuracy alongside flawed clinical reasoning**, and **few tools target musculoskeletal (MSK) triage well; many perform poorly on MSK populations**, so output should **support but not replace** clinical judgment. Broader dialogue-system and chatbot literature provides important context: **deep learning has made data-driven dialogue increasingly feasible**, but **engineering-heavy approaches still dominate practical deployments**; chatbot architectures rest on **pattern matching and machine learning** as core technologies, with **significant room for improvement before truly intelligent behavior**; and while **LLMs like ChatGPT hold immense promise in healthcare**, they **require further research** and current limitations in medical contexts are acknowledged.

### ii) Gaps and opportunities

There is a clear gap between **development** of prediction tools and **prospective evidence that their use improves outcomes, efficiency, or safety** in real pathways. Opportunities include: rigorous external validation and calibration reporting; trials that embed tools in workflow and measure harms, equity, and resource use; **MSK-specific** triage and education tools with transparent performance in MSK cohorts; regulatory clarity and **standardized, comparable evaluation methods** for digital triage, including **setting-specific evaluations, regulation and standardization of assessments, and patient-centered design**; research that shifts AI from **classification toward treatment support, prognosis under intervention, and mechanistic insight**; and **reproducible ML** (shared code, preregistered benchmarks) plus explicit testing of whether **apparent LLM accuracy reflects safe reasoning** for deployment decisions. The dialogue-systems literature highlights that **transfer learning between datasets and incorporation of external knowledge remain underexplored** opportunities for health applications, and that bridging the gap between **data-driven dialogue methods and clinical deployment** requires attention to both technical maturity and domain-specific risks.
