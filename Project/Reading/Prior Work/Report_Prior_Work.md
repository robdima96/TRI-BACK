## 2. Comparison of stratified primary care management for LBP with current best practice (STarT Back): a randomised controlled trial

**First Author:** Jonathan C. Hill

**Summary:**

At four months, the **stratified-care group improved more on disability scores compared to usual care** (adjusted mean difference about 1.8 points on the Roland-Morris scale), and a **smaller but still significant gap remained at twelve months (about 1.1 points)**, matching **small-to-moderate effect** sizes. By twelve months the intervention also yielded roughly 0.04 extra quality-adjusted life years per person and lower back-pain-related costs in the analysis shown.

**Tool:** STarT

**Date:** 2011-10-29
**Publication:** Lancet
**Volume:** 378

## 3. Effect of Stratified Care for LBP in Family Practice (IMPaCT Back): A Prospective Population-Based Sequential Comparison

**First Author:** Nadine E. Foster

**Summary:**

**Stratified care produced a statistically significant but small average disability improvement versus usual care (mean Roland-Morris difference about 0.7 points), with a much larger effect in the high-risk subgroup (about 2.3 points)**. Workers took about half as many sick days (median 4 vs 8), sick notes dropped (9% vs 15%), and the economic analysis suggested cost savings alongside small utility gains.

**Tool:** IMPaCT

**Date:** 2014/03/01
**Publication:** The Annals of Family Medicine
**Volume:** 12
**Issue:** 2
**Pages:** 102-111
**DOI:** 10.1370/afm.1625 <http://doi.org/10.1370/afm.1625>

## 4. Estimating the Risk of Chronic Pain: Development and Validation of a Prognostic Model (PICKUP) for Patients with Acute LBP

**First Author:** Adrian C. Traeger

**Summary:**

In external validation data **the PICKUP model discriminated people who developed chronic pain from those who did not with moderate accuracy (AUC about 0.66 with a 95% confidence interval excluding 0.5)**. Decision-curve analysis suggested risk-based screening could avoid many unnecessary interventions compared with treating everyone the same, though **calibration still drifted for some high-risk groups**.

**Tool:** PICKUP

## 7. Usability Testing of a Digital Assessment Routing Tool for Musculoskeletal Disorders: Iterative, Convergent Mixed Methods Study

**First Author:** Cabella Lowe

**Summary:**

Twenty-two participants tested four DART iterations over five rounds (50 clinical presentations). Usability problems fell from 12 to zero; **mean System Usability Scale score was 84.3 (excellent range)**. Mean completion time was 5.2 minutes with no DART system errors. Trust and intention to act improved during the study. **The authors concluded serious usability barriers were resolved, supporting planned RCTs of safety and effectiveness versus usual care**.

## 8. Risk-based stratified primary care for common musculoskeletal pain presentations (STarT MSK): a cluster-randomised, controlled trial

## 13. Diagnostic accuracy of a large language model in rheumatology: comparison of physician and ChatGPT-4

**First Author:** Martin Krusche

**Summary:**

On the Gräf et al. (2022) case set, ChatGPT-4 matched the top diagnosis as often as rheumatologists (35% vs 39%; not significant) and was similar for top-three diagnoses (60% vs 55%). In inflammatory rheumatic disease (IRD) cases, ChatGPT-4 led on top diagnosis (71% vs 62%) and top-three (86% vs 74%). In non-IRD cases, physicians had higher top-diagnosis accuracy (27% vs 15%). Considering only the first diagnostic suggestion, ChatGPT-4 classified 58% of cases as IRD vs 56% for rheumatologists (not significant). **The pattern shows higher sensitivity for IRD with lower specificity in non-IRD cases, with ChatGPT matching or outperforming rheumatologists**.

**Tool:** ChatGPT-4

## Overall synthesis (from Summaries, emphasizing bolded conclusions)

### i) Main findings

**Stratified primary care for LBP** has **mixed trial evidence**: classic STarT Back and IMPaCT reports describe **greater disability improvement than usual care** (small to moderate effects, with **larger benefits in higher-risk subgroups** in IMPaCT) and **economic or work-related advantages** in some analyses. Later or different implementations **do not always replicate**: **STarT MSK showed no meaningful difference on primary pain and function outcomes**; a **classification-based pathway was not superior on main physical function at twelve months** (with some secondary/process differences); and the **SupportPrim personalized decision-support trial did not show benefit on primary end points** and **did worse on one function scale** than usual care. **Prognostic and machine-learning models** (e.g., PICKUP, acute-LBP non-recovery, chronic pain decision trees) typically reach **modest discrimination (often around AUC 0.66 or mid-0.6s in validation or cross-validation)** with **calibration limitations** or **external validation still required**. **Digital and AI-augmented tools** show a split pattern: **selfBACK** produced a **statistically significant but modest disability difference** of **uncertain clinical importance**; **NLP-augmented triage** can **raise F1 for some referral categories** while **overall accuracy stays modest and below standalone use**; **referral-letter screening** in other work reaches **moderate to good AUC for some conditions** in multi-site validation. **Case-based reasoning** improved **device configuration success** in one application; **SupportPrim** similarity retrieval aligned **reasonably with prognostic questionnaire distances** in development work. **DART** progressed from **excellent usability** and **pilot triage agreement (weak to moderate raw, higher service-adjusted concordance, no harmful episodes)** to a **quality-improvement cohort** reporting **high clinician–tool agreement, operational savings, and high satisfaction**—contrasting with **null or negative randomized evidence** for some other digital decision-support rollouts. **DeSSBack** pilots suggested **medium effects on disability and anxiety** and **positive clinician views**. **LLM diagnostic benchmarking** (rheumatology) showed **ChatGPT-4 matching or exceeding specialists in some metrics** with a **sensitivity–specificity trade-off** by case type.

### ii) Gaps and opportunities

The field shows **inconsistent effectiveness of stratified and AI-supported pathways** across settings, tools, and trial designs, so **generalization from positive early trials is unsafe**. Gaps include: **definitive trials** of tools such as **DART** and **SupportPrim** against **hard patient-centered outcomes**; bridging **strong service-level metrics** (agreement, throughput) to **long-term safety, appropriateness, and equity**; **prospective external validation** of ML prognostic and triage models with **decision curve or management consequences**; and **integrating NLP and LLM components** where **overall triage accuracy remains modest**, including guardrails when **specialist-level diagnostic scores mask downstream reasoning or referral appropriateness**. Opportunities lie in **head-to-head comparisons** of stratified care versus **digitally augmented** pathways, **implementation science** on fidelity (noted as weak for patients in one CDS pilot), and **transparent reporting** when **pilot success does not match later RCT null results**.
